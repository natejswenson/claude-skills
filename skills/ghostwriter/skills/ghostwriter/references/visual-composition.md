# Compose the picture before the copy

For Codex imagegen cards, prefer a visual explanation or editorial illustration
over a typographic poster. Keep the existing 12-row visual review and current
brand laws unchanged. This guides composition; it adds no score or waiver.

1. Identify what the reader can **see**: a relationship, comparison, sequence,
   missing input, or consequence. Choose the visual form before writing a
   headline. A relevant illustration, meaningful repeated symbols, spatial
   comparison, or a clear diagram can carry it. Do not add clip art to a text
   layout and call that a visual explanation.
2. Give the picture most of the canvas when its information supports it, roughly half to two thirds. Keep the
   headline at supporting scale, usually one short line or two modest lines.
   A giant numeral, word, or status label is still typography; it does not
   satisfy a request for more imagery. Use a type-led card when the user asks
   for one or when the content specifically needs a typographic treatment.
   This is a composition guide, not an area quota: do not enlarge plain blocks or
   repeat symbols just to fill it. For a simple comparison, precise scales and
   thoughtful spacing can be more compelling than a large pictogram grid.
3. Write only the labels needed to interpret the picture and preserve material
   conditions. Move prose back into the post instead of shrinking it into
   microcopy. Necessary qualifications, identity and accessible labels stay.
4. Apply a muted-text test: if the words were ignored, would the image still
   communicate a meaningful relationship or recognizable subject? An abstract
   chart may rely on labels to identify its subject; its marks must still convey
   comparison, sequence or structure. Do not add literal illustration just to
   make the subject recognizable. If only type carries the relationship, redesign
   the hero. Removing a picture should lose something more than decoration.
5. Translate the subject into PRESS: purposeful ink linework, silhouette or
   restrained engraving on paper, clear geometry and generous space. Keep the
   existing palette, type roles, stamp, rules and accent limits. Illustrating a
   real subject does not permit gradients, shadows, filled UI panels, stock AI
   symbols, or meaningless ornament.
   Favor crisp, considered typography and a clean surface. Distress and extreme
   condensed headlines are not brand requirements. Do not use artificial grain
   to hide soft output, or treat a matching palette as evidence of finished craft.
6. Ground what the picture implies. Label illustrative counts or scenes as
   examples; never present them as the user's actual readings or experience.
   Distinguish an unavailable comparison from a missing measurement. Arrows,
   counts, scales and groupings still need the same evidence and pixel review.

In the prompt receipt, describe the `visual_encoding`: which marks carry the
meaning, how they map to the evidence, what labels resolve ambiguity, and the
intended picture/text balance. When revising a text-heavy card, compare the
actual old and new images to confirm that the composition changed, not just the
word count. The existing substance, composition, hierarchy and originality rows
judge the result; all other visual checks still apply.

Correcting a chart's arithmetic is not necessarily a design improvement. A real
generated comparison replaced inaccurate bars with two rows of large plain squares;
the labels and counts became correct, but the user rejected its visual quality.
Use this as a failure example, not a ban on unit charts: marks need purposeful
proportion, refined linework, type and spacing, and a compelling whole. If the
composition is still a generic worksheet, recompose it instead of adding ornament.
